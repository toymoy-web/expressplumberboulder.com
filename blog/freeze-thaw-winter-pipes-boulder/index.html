Freeze-Thaw Season: Protecting Boulder Pipes Each Winter

By the Boulder Express Plumbing team.

Talk to a Plumber.
(303) 552-3896
Call Now

Why This Pattern Repeats Every Year.

Every winter, we see the same pattern: a stretch of relatively mild weather followed by a sudden, hard overnight cold snap, and a spike in frozen and burst pipe calls the next morning. That's not a coincidence. A hard, sudden freeze after warmer weather is exactly the condition that catches exposed or uninsulated pipe sections off guard.

Water expands as it freezes, and if it's trapped inside a pipe with nowhere to go, that expansion can split the pipe wall. The break itself often doesn't show up until the ice thaws and the water starts flowing again, which is why a lot of these calls come in the day after a cold snap, not during it.

Where Pipes Actually Freeze.

It's rarely the pipes you'd think of first. The most common freeze points in Boulder homes are the ones exposed to unconditioned space.

Crawlspaces with limited insulation around supply lines.

Unheated additions or converted garages.

Pipes routed along exterior walls, especially on the north side of a house.

Outdoor hose bibs and irrigation lines that weren't fully winterized before the first hard freeze.

What to Do the Moment You Suspect a Freeze.

If a faucet is only trickling or producing no water at all after a cold night, that's the first sign a section of pipe upstream has frozen. If you can locate the frozen section and it's accessible, controlled heat from a hair dryer or heat tape is a safe way to thaw it. Never use a propane torch or other open flame, especially near wood framing or insulation, since that turns a plumbing problem into a genuine fire risk.

If a pipe has already split, shutting off the main water valve immediately is the single most useful thing you can do before help arrives. It stops the situation from getting worse while you wait, and our frozen pipe repair page covers what to expect once a technician gets there, including whether the section needs simple thawing or full burst pipe repair.

Reducing Your Risk Before the Next Cold Snap.

Homes in exposed or older parts of the city benefit most from a proactive check before winter really sets in. That's especially true in neighborhoods like Gunbarrel, where some crawlspace and utility configurations from the original 1970s-80s construction wave weren't necessarily built with today's insulation standards in mind. Insulating exposed pipe sections and disconnecting outdoor hoses before the first hard freeze are simple steps that meaningfully reduce the odds of a middle-of-the-night emergency call. None of this requires professional help to do yourself, it just requires actually doing it before the weather turns, which is the part that most often gets postponed until it's already too late.

What Boulder's Weather Pattern Means for Timing.

Boulder's weather along the Front Range is known for exactly the kind of dramatic swings that cause this problem: a genuinely warm day followed by an overnight temperature drop of 40 degrees or more isn't unusual here. That volatility is worth building into your own seasonal routine, checking exposed pipe insulation each fall rather than assuming last year's cold snap was a one-time event. Treat it as a recurring seasonal task, not something you only think about after the first bad night.

If You're Leaving Town During Winter.

An empty house is at higher freeze risk than an occupied one, since nobody is there to notice a heating system failure or a draft finding its way to an exposed pipe. If you're traveling for an extended period during winter, keeping the thermostat set no lower than the mid-50s, rather than turning it off entirely, and having a neighbor check on the house periodically are both reasonable precautions worth taking before you leave. A smart thermostat that alerts you to unusual temperature drops while you're away adds another layer of warning if something does go wrong in your absence.

After the Repair, Prevent a Repeat.

Once a frozen or burst pipe is actually fixed, it's worth asking why that specific section froze in the first place rather than just moving on. If it was an exposed section in a crawlspace, adding pipe insulation or heat tape to that specific run is a small investment compared to another emergency call next winter. If the freeze happened in a section that's been a recurring problem, rerouting that line away from the exterior wall entirely is sometimes the more permanent fix.

Documenting what happened, including which section failed and roughly how cold it got before it did, also helps with any insurance claim for resulting water damage. It also gives us useful context if you ever call about a similar issue in a different part of the same home.

Common Questions.

How cold does it need to get for pipes to freeze in Boulder?

It depends heavily on how exposed the specific pipe section is, but a sudden overnight drop well below freezing, especially after a warmer stretch, is the classic trigger we see most often.

Should I leave a faucet dripping during a hard freeze?

For a pipe you know is at risk, a slow trickle can relieve pressure and reduce freeze risk in that specific line. It's a reasonable precaution for a known vulnerable pipe, not a substitute for insulating exposed sections in the first place.

Can frozen pipes burst even if I never see them freeze?

Yes. A pipe inside a wall or crawlspace can freeze and split without any visible sign until it thaws and water starts flowing again, which is exactly why prevention matters more than watching for symptoms.
