Why Does My Drain Keep Clogging? Root Intrusion in Boulder's Older Sewer Lines

By the Boulder Express Plumbing team.

Call Now

The Pattern That Points to Roots, Not a New Clog.

If a drain has been cabled and cleared, only for the same clog to return a few weeks later, that pattern is worth paying attention to. A brand-new, unrelated clog showing up in exactly the same spot repeatedly is unlikely. What's far more common is root intrusion, tree roots that have found their way into the pipe through a joint or small crack and continue growing back after each cabling clears the immediate blockage.

Why Older Boulder Sewer Lines Are Especially Prone to This.

Neighborhoods like North Boulder and Downtown Boulder and University Hill still have original clay sewer laterals in places, decades old, with joints that have had plenty of time to develop small gaps. Tree roots are drawn to the moisture inside these lines, and once a root finds its way in through even a small opening, it continues growing and expanding that opening over time.

This is a slow, ongoing process rather than a single event, which is exactly why cabling alone doesn't solve it permanently. A cable clears a path through the root mass sitting in the pipe, but it doesn't remove the roots growing into the pipe wall itself, so they grow back into that same cleared path within weeks or months.

Why Hydro Jetting Works Where Cabling Doesn't.

High-pressure water from hydro jetting clears the full diameter of the pipe rather than just punching a path through the middle of a blockage. For root intrusion specifically, that means actually clearing root growth off the pipe wall rather than leaving it in place to regrow into the same channel a cable just cut through.

Before recommending jetting, we typically run a sewer camera inspection first, since jetting an already-compromised pipe at the wrong pressure can cause damage if the pipe's actual condition isn't confirmed beforehand. The camera also tells us whether we're dealing with root intrusion at a single joint or a more widespread pattern along the line's length.

When It's More Than a Cleaning Can Fix.

If root intrusion has been going on long enough, it can crack or offset the pipe itself rather than just growing through an existing joint gap. In that case, jetting clears the immediate blockage but doesn't address the structural damage causing it, and a spot repair or fuller line assessment becomes the more honest recommendation. We'll always tell you which situation you're actually in based on what the camera shows, not just recommend the more expensive option by default.

Trees You'd Never Suspect.

It's not always the tree directly in your own yard. Roots travel well beyond a tree's visible canopy in search of moisture. A sewer lateral can pick up intrusion from a neighbor's tree, a street tree in the public right-of-way, or landscaping planted decades ago by a previous owner that's since grown considerably larger than the original planting. This is part of why the actual source of root intrusion is often a surprise to homeowners who assumed their own yard was clear of the culprit. A camera inspection settles the question definitively rather than leaving it as a guess about which tree is actually responsible. It's a reasonable thing to bring up with a neighbor directly once you actually know for certain which specific tree, growing on which specific neighboring property, is truly and fully responsible for the root intrusion actually happening inside the line. That certainty is worth having before any conversation with a neighbor about it.

How Often This Actually Needs Attention.

There's no fixed schedule that applies to every property, since it depends on the specific trees nearby, the pipe's age and joint condition, and how the line was previously treated. A line that's had jetting done once with a camera-confirmed clean result may go years without another issue. One with ongoing intrusion from a large, well-established tree may need more frequent attention. We'll give you an honest read on what your specific line's history suggests, rather than a generic recommendation that doesn't reflect your actual situation.

Common Questions.

How can I tell if my recurring clog is roots and not something else?

The clearest sign is timing: a clog that returns weeks or months after cabling, in the same general location, points strongly to root regrowth. A camera inspection confirms it definitively rather than leaving it as a guess.

Can I prevent root intrusion without replacing the whole line?

Periodic hydro jetting on a schedule can manage root intrusion for years in a line that's otherwise structurally sound, though it's ongoing maintenance rather than a permanent fix if the pipe joints continue to have small openings roots can find.

Are certain trees more likely to cause root intrusion than others?

Trees with aggressive, water-seeking root systems, like willows and cottonwoods, are more commonly associated with sewer line intrusion than trees with shallower root patterns, though any tree near an older line with a joint gap is a potential source over enough time.

Does removing the tree stop root intrusion for good?

It stops new root growth from that specific tree, but roots already inside the pipe don't disappear on their own and still need to be cleared. Removal helps prevent the problem from recurring, but it isn't a substitute for addressing what's already inside the line, and nearby trees can still eventually become a new source of the exact same problem over the following years.
