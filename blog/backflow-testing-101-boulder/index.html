Backflow Testing 101: What Boulder Property Owners Need to Know

By the Boulder Express Plumbing team.

Talk to a Plumber.
(303) 552-3896
Call Now

What Backflow Prevention Actually Does.

Backflow prevention exists to stop contaminated water from siphoning backward into the clean municipal water supply, which can happen if pressure in a connected system, like an irrigation line, temporarily drops below the pressure in the main line. A backflow preventer is a mechanical device designed specifically to stop that reverse flow, and testing confirms it's actually still doing its job.

Who Actually Needs This in Boulder.

Properties with an irrigation system are the most common case, and Boulder's landscaping-heavy residential and commercial properties mean this applies more broadly than people expect. Certain commercial connections, including some fire suppression systems and specific plumbing fixtures, also require a backflow preventer and the accompanying annual test. If you're not sure whether your property has one, that's worth confirming rather than assuming it doesn't apply to you.

What the Annual Test Actually Involves.

Testing is quick and non-invasive: a technician checks the device under pressure to confirm it prevents reverse flow the way it's designed to, usually completed in under an hour for a single-device residential property. If a device fails, we can typically repair or replace it on the same visit and provide the certification paperwork your utility requires, which we file directly with the appropriate authority in most cases.

Preparing for the Test.

You don't need to do much to prepare beyond making sure the device is accessible. If it's behind landscaping that's grown over it, or inside a locked mechanical room, clearing access ahead of time saves the technician from having to work around obstacles that could have been cleared in advance. Beyond that, there's no special preparation required on your end, and the process itself is quick enough that it rarely disrupts your day or requires you to take significant time away from other plans.

For commercial properties, testing sometimes involves multiple devices across different connections, which our commercial plumbing page covers alongside other routine commercial service needs.

What Happens If You Skip It.

Water utilities that require backflow testing generally follow up when a property's certification lapses, and continued non-compliance can eventually affect your water service. Beyond the compliance angle, a device that's actually failing and goes untested isn't protecting the water supply the way it's supposed to, which is the entire reason the requirement exists in the first place. Our backflow testing and prevention page covers the full testing and certification process if you're due, or if you're installing a new irrigation system and need a device set up for the first time.

New Irrigation Installations Need This Too.

If you're adding an irrigation system to a Boulder property that doesn't currently have one, a backflow preventer isn't an optional add-on, it's generally required as part of the connection approval before your utility signs off on the installation. This is worth building into your landscaping project's timeline and budget from the start rather than discovering it as an unexpected requirement partway through.

What a Failed Test Actually Means.

A device that fails testing isn't necessarily a sign of neglect, mechanical components like backflow preventers wear out over time through normal use, the same way any valve or moving part eventually does. What matters is addressing it promptly once a failure is identified, since the device isn't providing the protection it's designed for until it's repaired or replaced. We can typically handle that on the same visit as the test itself, so a failed test doesn't turn into a second appointment and a second trip charge.

Choosing the Right Device for Your Connection.

Not every backflow preventer is interchangeable across connection types. Irrigation systems, fire suppression lines, and commercial kitchen connections each have different flow and pressure requirements, and a device sized or rated for the wrong application won't provide reliable protection even if it's technically installed correctly. This matters most for new installations, where getting the right device type from the start avoids a failed inspection down the line.

Keeping Your Own Records.

Beyond whatever documentation your utility keeps, it's worth holding onto your own copy of each year's test certification. If you ever sell the property, a documented history of compliant annual testing is a small but genuine point in your favor during due diligence, and it saves you from having to reconstruct the record if a question comes up later.

Common Questions.

How do I know if my property already has a backflow preventer installed?

If you have an irrigation system, it's very likely, but the only way to know for certain is to have someone check. We can identify and assess an existing device as part of a routine visit.

Is backflow testing something I need to schedule every single year?

For most properties with a device, yes, most Boulder-area utilities require annual certification. Confirming your specific requirement with your utility is worth doing if you've never had it explained directly.

Can I test my own backflow preventer to save money?

Certified backflow testing generally needs to be performed and documented by a qualified technician for the certification to be accepted by your utility, so a self-test wouldn't satisfy the actual requirement even if you have the right equipment.

What does a backflow preventer actually look like on my property?

For a residential irrigation connection, it's typically a small assembly of valves and a test cock mounted above ground near where the irrigation line branches off. It's often enclosed in a small box for weather protection, usually near where the irrigation system's main shutoff is located too.
