Renovating an Older Boulder Home? Check the Plumbing First

By the Boulder Express Plumbing team.

Talk to a Plumber.
(303) 552-3896
Call Now

Why Timing Matters More Than People Think.

The best time to address a plumbing issue in an older home is before a renovation, not after. Once new drywall, flooring, or cabinetry goes in, any plumbing problem that was hiding behind the old finishes becomes dramatically more expensive to fix, because now you're undoing finished work to get at it.

What Tends to Hide in Older Boulder Homes.

Homes built before roughly 1960, common in neighborhoods like North Boulder and Mapleton Hill, sometimes still have original galvanized supply lines or clay sewer laterals that have never been touched since construction. These aren't necessarily failing right now, but a renovation is exactly the moment to find out, since the walls and floor are already open in a way they won't be again for years.

Galvanized supply lines with internal corrosion that hasn't yet caused a visible symptom.

Sewer laterals with early-stage root intrusion at aging pipe joints.

Water heaters vented into a shared masonry chimney, relevant if the renovation touches that space.

Mixed pipe materials from past piecemeal repairs, which is common after decades of small updates.

What a Pre-Renovation Check Actually Involves.

A pre-renovation plumbing check isn't about assuming something is wrong. It's about confirming the actual condition of what's there before it's sealed up again. That can mean a sewer camera inspection if the renovation touches drain lines, or a general walkthrough under general plumbing repair if the scope is more about fixtures and supply lines.

The goal is straightforward: catch anything worth addressing while it's still accessible and inexpensive to fix, rather than discovering it three years later when it fails behind brand-new tile.

Renovation-Specific Plumbing Decisions.

A kitchen or bathroom remodel is also a natural moment to make upgrades that are much harder to justify outside of a renovation, like relocating a water heater, adding a water softener, or correcting a fixture that's always had inadequate water pressure. These are the kinds of decisions that are cheap during a renovation and expensive as standalone projects afterward.

Coordinating Timing With Your Contractor.

The biggest practical challenge in a renovation isn't the plumbing check itself, it's coordinating the timing so the plumbing work happens at the right point in the broader project. Ideally, any camera inspection or diagnostic walkthrough happens before demolition begins, so findings can actually influence the renovation plan rather than showing up as a surprise mid-project.

If your contractor hasn't already built a plumbing check into the schedule, it's worth raising the question directly rather than assuming it's covered. A short conversation upfront about what's behind the walls can save both time and money once the project is underway.

What This Costs Compared to Waiting.

A pre-renovation plumbing check is a small cost relative to the renovation itself, and it's a fraction of what the same discovery costs if it's made after finishes are installed. Reopening a wall or floor to fix a plumbing issue that was missed during a renovation typically costs several times more than it would have during the original project. That's simply because you're now undoing finished work in addition to fixing the underlying problem, and matching finishes years later is rarely as simple as it sounds.

A Real Example of Why This Matters.

Consider a kitchen remodel in a pre-1960s Boulder home where the original galvanized supply line was never inspected before new tile and cabinetry went in. If that line starts leaking a year later, fixing it now means cutting into brand-new tile and possibly removing cabinetry that was custom-fit to the space. The same inspection, done before the renovation, would have caught the issue while the wall was already open at no additional cost to access it.

Bring This Up With Your Contractor Directly.

Not every general contractor automatically includes a plumbing-specific inspection in their renovation scope, especially for smaller projects. It's worth asking directly whether one is planned, and if not, arranging it yourself before demolition starts. A short conversation now is a lot cheaper than discovering a problem after the drywall is already up. Most contractors are receptive to the request once you explain the reasoning, since it protects their own project timeline too, and a delay found before demolition is far easier to absorb than one found mid-project when trades are already scheduled and materials are on order.

Common Questions.

Should I get a plumbing inspection even if nothing seems wrong before I renovate?

Yes, especially in a home built before the 1960s. Original galvanized or clay pipe often shows no symptoms until it actually fails, and a renovation is the cheapest possible time to find and address it.

Does a pre-renovation plumbing check delay the project?

Usually not significantly. A walkthrough or camera inspection typically takes an hour or two and can often be scheduled to align with the renovation's existing timeline rather than adding a separate delay.

Who should pay for a pre-renovation plumbing inspection, me or the contractor?

This varies by project and by contractor, so it's worth clarifying upfront rather than assuming. Some contractors build it into their estimate, while others expect the homeowner to arrange it separately.

Does a plumbing inspection before renovation require a permit?

No, an inspection itself is diagnostic and doesn't require a permit. Any actual repair work uncovered by the inspection follows the normal permitting rules for that type of work, which your contractor should already be factoring into the overall project timeline and budget from the very start of planning.
